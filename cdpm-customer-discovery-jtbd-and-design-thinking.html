Question 1 of 6 · Customer Discovery, JTBD, and Design Thinking
A JTBD interview reveals that a small-business owner actively wants to switch from spreadsheets to a new invoicing SaaS tool (she has clearly articulated both the pain driving her away from spreadsheets and the appeal of the new tool). Yet six months later she still hasn't switched, and further probing reveals she worries the new tool won't integrate with her existing accounting software and that data migration could introduce billing errors. Using the Four Forces of Progress model, which force is preventing her from switching?
Anxiety of the new solution is the force representing fear, uncertainty, or perceived risk about adopting the new option — here, integration failure and migration errors are specific anxieties blocking the switch even though push and pull already exist.
Question 2 of 6 · Customer Discovery, JTBD, and Design Thinking
In Teresa Torres' opportunity-solution tree used for continuous product discovery, which sequence correctly represents the hierarchy from the top of the tree to the bottom?
The tree starts with a single desired Outcome at the root, branches into customer Opportunities (unmet needs/pain points) that could drive that outcome, then Solutions that address each opportunity, and finally Assumption Tests/Experiments that validate each solution's riskiest assumptions.
Question 3 of 6 · Customer Discovery, JTBD, and Design Thinking
Which of the following correctly follows the 'job story' format used in JTBD-based discovery, as opposed to a traditional user story?
Job stories follow the structure 'When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]' — this option leads with the triggering situation rather than a persona label, matching JTBD's emphasis on circumstance over demographic identity.
Question 4 of 6 · Customer Discovery, JTBD, and Design Thinking
According to continuous discovery habits popularized in modern JTBD-driven product management (Teresa Torres), what is the recommended minimum cadence for the product trio (PM, designer, engineer) to conduct direct customer touchpoints in order to sustain continuous discovery?
Continuous discovery habits require weekly customer touchpoints by the product trio as a standing habit, not an occasional event, so that opportunities and assumptions are continuously surfaced and tested rather than discovered only in bursts.
Question 5 of 6 · Customer Discovery, JTBD, and Design Thinking
A team wants to validate whether users understand an entirely new navigation concept for a mobile app before investing any time in visual design, branding, or micro-interactions. Which Design Thinking prototyping approach BEST fits this discovery goal?
Low-fidelity prototypes let teams test structural and conceptual understanding (navigation, IA, flow) cheaply and quickly, without visual polish distracting from or biasing the test of the underlying concept — exactly matching the stated goal.
Question 6 of 6 · Customer Discovery, JTBD, and Design Thinking
How does a service blueprint differ from a customer journey map in Design Thinking-driven discovery?
A journey map visualizes the customer's front-stage experience across stages and touchpoints, while a service blueprint extends that same timeline with back-stage elements — internal processes, systems, and support roles — that make the front-stage experience possible.
